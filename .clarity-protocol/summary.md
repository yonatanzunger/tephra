# Notebook *(working name)*

Some people think by talking. This one thinks by writing — which means that where the writing happens, and how it feels to do, isn't a matter of preference but a direct input to the quality of the thinking. The evidence is twenty years long and unusually clear: a physical lab notebook worked until the work started happening away from it; a structured version of that notebook, with a TODO list in the front and a calendar in the back, worked *excellently* until the same access problem caught up with it; and Google Docs and then M365 solved access and failed anyway, because they had less structure than paper and a mobile app that barely functioned. **None of them failed for lack of features.** The one that worked best had four status symbols and a calendar section.

So the target here is smaller than "a powerful notes app," and sharper. It's one continuous stream of writing — prose, equations, tables, figures pasted in place — that's always available on whichever device is at hand, a genuine pleasure to read and type into, and searchable months later when you half-remember something. Instead of maintaining separate notebooks per project, which has never once been worth the overhead, you select a passage after the fact and tag it with a subject; a subject view is just a filter over the one stream. The same select-a-range gesture also branches a passage into its own file, prints it, or flags it to come back to. Around that sit the things that always accreted around the notebook anyway: a TODO list that ranks by urgency instead of asking you to scan, a few pinned lists you keep returning to, a calendar of the trips and talks that matter, and collections of documents and URLs gathered while researching something.

Two things make it more interesting than a personal tool usually is. The first is a small discovery about why the paper notebook prevented projects from quietly dying: when a two-page spread filled up, you had to copy the still-live items forward, and everything you didn't copy died — cheaply, visibly, by a decision rather than by neglect. Digital lists never fill, so that review never happens, and things rot instead. Finding the digital equivalent of a page running out is an open and genuinely interesting design problem. The second is that the whole thing has to stay in plain, boring, durable files, so that the twenty years of thinking inside it never becomes hostage to whether this particular program still runs.

It's built for one person, on a Mac and an Android phone, by that same person in the evenings — which is the main constraint on how large it's allowed to get.
